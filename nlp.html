What an embedding is

🧒 In plain English

An embedding is a GPS coordinate for meaning. Words, sentences, products or customers that mean or behave alike get coordinates close together. "Refund" and "chargeback" sit near each other; "refund" and "mortgage" are far apart. Once everything has coordinates, "find similar" is just "find nearby points".

📐 Cosine similarity
cos(a, b) = a·b / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

It ranges from −1 to 1 and ignores vector length, only direction counts. If you L2-normalise vectors first, cosine similarity is just the dot product. That's why vector databases often store normalised embeddings.

Tokenisation: text → integer IDs

  • Word-level: huge vocabulary, and any word not in it becomes [UNK].
  • Character-level: no unknown words, but very long sequences.
  • Subword (BPE in GPT, WordPiece in BERT, SentencePiece in T5/Llama): frequent words stay whole, rare ones split: "chargebacks" → "charge", "back", "s". This is the standard today and handles typos, new words and Hinglish reasonably well.
⚠️ Gotcha

Always use the same tokenizer the model was pretrained with. Token count ≠ word count. That matters for context limits and LLM API cost (about 1.3 tokens per English word, more for Indic languages).

Word2Vec: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps"

🧒 In plain English

Slide a window over billions of sentences. Train a tiny network to predict the neighbouring words from a word (skip-gram), or a word from its neighbours (CBOW). The network's weight matrix becomes the embedding table. Words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors, and the famous result is king − man + woman ≈ queen.

Negative sampling makes it cheap. Instead of a softmax over the whole vocabulary, it learns to tell real (word, context) pairs from a few random fake pairs. The same trick powers item2vec and product2vec in e-commerce (sessions are "sentences", products are "words").

🎯 The limitation interviewers ask about

Static embeddings give "bank" one vector whether you mean a river bank or HDFC Bank. Contextual models (ELMo → BERT) fix this: the vector depends on the whole sentence.

Contextual and sentence embeddings

BERT-style token embeddings

Each token's vector depends on the sentence. Fine-tune them for classification and NER.

Sentence embeddings (SBERT, E5, BGE)

One vector per sentence, trained with contrastive learning: pull paraphrases together, push unrelated sentences apart. Use for semantic search, dedup, clustering and RAG retrieval.

📐 Contrastive (InfoNCE) loss in words

For each anchor, make its similarity to the positive high relative to all the negatives in the batch: L = −log( esim(a,p)/τ / Σj esim(a,j)/τ ). Bigger batches give more negatives and usually better embeddings. The same loss trains CLIP and two-tower recommenders.

TF-IDF vs fine-tuned BERT vs LLM: how to choose

✅ Decision rule
  1. TF-IDF + logistic regression: always the baseline. Fast, explainable, often 85–90% as good.
  2. Fine-tuned small encoder (DistilBERT, MiniLM, DeBERTa-small): when you have about 1k+ labels per class and need low latency and cost at high volume.
  3. LLM zero/few-shot: few or no labels, many classes that change often, or you need reasoning or extraction. Watch cost per call, latency and data privacy.
  4. LLM-labelled data → distil into a small encoder: get LLM quality at encoder cost. A very common industry pattern.

Interview Q&A

Word2Vec vs BERT embeddings?

Word2Vec gives one static vector per word, trained by local context prediction. It's cheap but can't separate senses. BERT gives contextual vectors from a deep bidirectional transformer, so "bank" differs by sentence. BERT is much better for downstream tasks but heavier.

Why subword tokenisation?

It balances vocabulary size and coverage: no out-of-vocabulary words, rare words composed from pieces, and shorter sequences than character-level. It handles misspellings and domain jargon gracefully.

Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder?

A bi-encoder embeds query and document separately, so document vectors can be precomputed and searched with ANN. It's fast and used for retrieval. A cross-encoder feeds the pair together through one transformer. It's more accurate but must run per pair, so it's used to re-rank the top 50–100 results.

Your complaint classifier has 30 classes and some have only 20 examples. What do you do?

Merge or hierarchy-group rare classes, use class-weighted loss, augment with paraphrases (back-translation or LLM-generated), try few-shot methods (SetFit, or LLM zero-shot for rare classes), and report macro-F1 and per-class recall so the rare classes aren't hidden by the averages.

How do you handle text longer than 512 tokens?

Chunk with overlap and aggregate (mean/max pool or attention over chunk embeddings), truncate smartly (head + tail often works), use long-context models (Longformer, modern LLMs), or summarise first.
